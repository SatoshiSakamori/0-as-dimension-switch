\begin{abstract}
We investigate a representation of nonzero real values in which positive
and negative quantities occupy separately labelled branches and the
approach to zero lies at an infinite end of a coordinate. We define nonzero arithmetic, then add a shared boundary element for
zero and continue calculations with ordinary real arithmetic. The
extended structure is isomorphic to the real numbers. It does not
remove singularities or introduce new physical laws.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation and scope}

In real arithmetic, zero is the additive identity, $a+0=a$, and is
absorbing under multiplication, $a\cdot0=0$. These rules are consistent
and are not a logical defect. Yet division by zero is undefined, and
zero often occurs at the boundary of expressions with divergent behavior.
This motivates asking whether zero can be represented as a boundary
outside a domain of ordinary nonzero values.

The conservative first step adopted here uses known signed logarithmic
coordinates. The term \emph{dimension switch} is a motivating metaphor
for a possible future boundary transition; it does not assert additional
physical spatial dimensions. No rule different from ordinary arithmetic is proposed: a sign-branch
change follows from the signed result of addition.

\section{The nonzero-value representation}

Define
\begin{equation}
 \Sstar=\{+1,-1\}\times\R,\qquad
 \Phi:\Sstar\longrightarrow\R\setminus\{0\},\qquad
 \Phi(s,u)=s e^u.
 \label{eq:phi}
\end{equation}
Here $s$ is a sign-state label and $u$ is a log-magnitude coordinate.
The inverse is well-defined:
\begin{equation}
 \Phi^{-1}(x)=\bigl(\operatorname{sgn}(x),\ln|x|\bigr),
 \qquad x\ne0.
\end{equation}
Thus $\Phi$ is a bijection. In this phase, the representation is
mathematically equivalent to the nonzero real numbers.

For either fixed sign,
\begin{equation}
 u\longrightarrow-\infty
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \Phi(s,u)\longrightarrow0\text{ along branch }s.
\end{equation}
The value zero is absent from $\Sstar$, and $-\infty$ is not a
finite real coordinate. Conversely, the coordinate $u=0$ is allowed:
it represents $x=s$. There are two ways to approach the missing
boundary, distinguished by $s$. In Section~\ref{sec:boundary}
we identify their limits with one shared boundary element.

\section{Arithmetic within the domain}

The usual nonzero multiplication and division are transported by
$\Phi$ as follows:
\begin{align}
 (s,u)\odot(t,v)&=(st,u+v),\\
 (s,u)\oslash(t,v)&=(st,u-v).
\end{align}
Both are defined for every input pair in $\Sstar\times\Sstar$.
The multiplicative identity is $(+1,0)$ and the multiplicative inverse
of $(s,u)$ is $(s,-u)$.
Hence $(\Sstar,\odot)$ is a group isomorphic to
$(\R\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$.

Sign reversal is
\begin{equation}
 N(s,u)=(-s,u),\qquad N(N(s,u))=(s,u).
\end{equation}
This algebraic involution is neither a continuous passage through
zero nor a physical reflection.

\subsection{Partial addition}

Since two nonzero real numbers can sum to zero, addition is not closed
on $\Sstar$. We therefore define a \emph{partial} operation:
\begin{equation}
(s,u)\boxplus(t,v)=
\begin{cases}
 \bigl(s,M+\ln(1+e^{m-M})\bigr),
  &s=t,\\[3pt]
 \bigl(s,u+\ln(1-e^{v-u})\bigr),
  &s\ne t,\ u>v,\\[3pt]
 \bigl(t,v+\ln(1-e^{u-v})\bigr),
  &s\ne t,\ v>u,
\end{cases}
\label{eq:add}
\end{equation}
where $M=\max(u,v)$ and $m=\min(u,v)$ in the first case.
When $s\ne t$ and $u=v$, the operation is \emph{undefined}.
For all defined inputs, substitution verifies
\begin{equation}
 \Phi(a\boxplus b)=\Phi(a)+\Phi(b).
\end{equation}
Subtraction can be performed as addition after sign reversal,
wherever the result remains nonzero.

For example $(+1,0)$ and $(-1,0)$ represent $1$ and $-1$.
Their conventional sum equals zero, and hence
\begin{equation}
 (+1,0)\boxplus(-1,0)
 \quad\text{is undefined in }\Sstar.
\end{equation}
This failure of closure occurs \emph{within $\Sstar$ only}.
In the next section a shared boundary is adjoined, completing
addition without changing its ordinary result. The extended
structure is isomorphic to the real numbers.

\section{A shared zero boundary and unchanged arithmetic}
\label{sec:boundary}

To represent the same boundary reached from both sign branches,
adjoin one special element $\mathbf{0}$:
\begin{equation}
 \overline{\mathcal S}=\Sstar\sqcup\{\mathbf{0}\},\qquad
 \widehat\Phi:\overline{\mathcal S}\longrightarrow\R,\qquad
 \widehat\Phi(s,u)=s e^u,\quad \widehat\Phi(\mathbf{0})=0.
\end{equation}
The boundary is not a finite coordinate pair $(s,u)$. The map
$\widehat\Phi$ is a bijection. We impose the intended rule:
\emph{arithmetic is unchanged in either sign branch}.
Define
\begin{align}
 a\boxplus b&=\widehat\Phi^{-1}
  \bigl(\widehat\Phi(a)+\widehat\Phi(b)\bigr),\\
 a\boxtimes b&=\widehat\Phi^{-1}
  \bigl(\widehat\Phi(a)\widehat\Phi(b)\bigr).
\end{align}
Addition and multiplication are now defined for every pair in
$\overline{\mathcal S}$. Subtraction means addition with sign
reversal, where $N(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}$; division is defined
only for a divisor unequal to $\mathbf{0}$.

Explicitly, cancellation yields a boundary value, and calculation
continues after that event:
\begin{align}
 (+1,0)\boxplus(-1,0)&=\mathbf{0},\\
 \mathbf{0}\boxplus(-1,0)&=(-1,0),\\
 (+1,0)\boxplus(-1,\ln 2)&=(-1,0),\\
 (-1,\ln 2)\boxplus(+1,\ln 3)&=(+1,0).
\end{align}
The third equation is the ordinary result $1-2=-1$ expressed
as positive magnitude one in the negative-sign branch.
At the shared boundary, a positive added value enters the positive
branch and a negative added value enters the negative branch.
Neither physical reflection nor an extra transition law is needed.

Equip the extended space with the metric
\begin{equation}
 d(a,b)=|\widehat\Phi(a)-\widehat\Phi(b)|.
\end{equation}
Then $\widehat\Phi$ is an isometry onto the real line.
In this topology both branches approach the same point:
\begin{equation}
 \lim_{u\to-\infty}(+1,u)=\mathbf{0},\qquad
 \lim_{u\to-\infty}(-1,u)=\mathbf{0}.
\end{equation}
Infinity is not a finite coordinate to be used in calculations;
exact cancellation returns $\mathbf{0}$ directly.

Since the operations were transported from the real numbers,
$(\overline{\mathcal S},\boxplus,\boxtimes)$ is a field
isomorphic to $(\R,+,\cdot)$, with the same algebraic laws.
The boundary still acts as the additive identity and absorbs
multiplication; division by it remains undefined. Thus the
boundary interpretation does not make zero intrinsically
nonnumeric or resolve mathematical or physical singularities.
No approach-direction history or additional state is stored.

\section{Limits and physical interpretation}

With $x=\Phi(s,u)=s e^u$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{1}{x}=s e^{-u}.
\end{equation}
Consequently reciprocal inversion becomes $(s,u)\mapsto(s,-u)$.
As $x\to0$ along a fixed sign branch, $u\to-\infty$ and the
reciprocal log-magnitude diverges to $+\infty$. The singular behavior
is \emph{not removed}; its coordinate description has changed.
Division by zero is still undefined.

One-sided behavior is not made unnecessary by the representation.
For instance,
\begin{equation}
 \lim_{x\to0^+}\frac1x=+\infty,\qquad
 \lim_{x\to0^-}\frac1x=-\infty.
\end{equation}
Separate sign states make the distinction explicit without
eliminating it.

Coordinate-dependent singularities can sometimes be removed by
better coordinates, whereas divergence of a coordinate-invariant
quantity cannot be fixed by relabelling a coordinate alone. No
physical consequences follow from the present representation without
a separate physical model and its invariants.

\section{Scope and remaining research questions}

This phase settles the shared-boundary choice and specifies how
calculations continue after cancellation, preserving exactly the
usual arithmetic laws on both branches. More elaborate state
memory, different boundary laws, or a new physical theory would
be separate hypotheses, not results of this model.

The next useful question is whether this particular coordinate
description helps with specific, coordinate-dependent
singularities. Coordinate-invariant divergences cannot be
removed by a mere change of representation. No bounce law,
magnitude-conservation claim, antimatter model, or
thermodynamic phase transition is asserted.

\section{Relation to established work}

Representing a nonzero real number by its sign and the logarithm
of its absolute value is a standard idea in signed logarithmic
number systems (LNS). Exact opposite-sign cancellation corresponds
to the logarithmic coordinate tending to negative infinity. After adding one shared zero boundary, the result is isomorphic
to the real field. The present construction therefore changes the
representation rather than creating a new algebra.
